The Hard Truth About SIP Lifespans: Why Most Investors Quit Early

Every month, AMFI publishes fresh SIP data — and every month, the same quiet pattern shows up in the numbers. It has nothing to do with market crashes. It has everything to do with who picked the fund.

~8x

Direct-plan SIP accounts fell by 34.7% year-on-year as of March 2026 (down to 17.55 lakh accounts from 26.87 lakh). Regular-plan SIP accounts, over the same period, fell by just 4.4%. Investors managing their own fund picks are discontinuing their SIPs at nearly eight times the rate of investors who go through an advisor.

Source: AMFI data, as reported by Deepak Wealth Framework, July 2026.

That number surprises most people, because the popular narrative runs the other way: "direct plans are for smart, disciplined investors; regular plans are for people who get sold to." The data says something more uncomfortable — self-directed investors are, on average, the ones walking away first.

Why does this happen?

It's rarely about the money running out. Industry commentary points to a specific behavioural pattern: DIY investors tend to pick funds based on trailing 1-3 year returns, then panic-sell or simply stop contributing the moment that fund has an ordinary, unremarkable year — which every fund eventually does. Markets don't move in straight lines, but a lot of direct-plan strategies are built as if they will.

This lines up with what SEBI itself has said publicly. At an NJ Partners/FICCI event in August 2026, SEBI whole-time member Amarjeet Singh noted that 34% of regular-plan SIPs cross the five-year mark, compared with just 20% of direct-plan SIPs. The gap isn't about fund quality — both plans hold the identical portfolio. It's about whether anyone is there to talk you out of quitting on a bad month.

But here's the part that matters more

None of this means every regular plan, or every person who sells one, is doing right by you. AMFI's own broader data shows the SIP stoppage ratio — the number of SIPs discontinued or completed against new registrations — has been running at 75-76% through most of 2026, and crossed 100% in March and April. Some of that is natural (SIPs completing their chosen tenure), but a meaningful part is investors who were sold something they didn't understand walking away once they realised it.

A specific warning: a bank counter is not an advisor

In June 2026, the RBI issued sweeping new directions on how banks market and sell financial products — effective January 2027 — describing the current system as "systematically designed to benefit the seller more than the buyer." This didn't come from nowhere. A well-documented pattern exists in Indian banking: relationship managers pushing New Fund Offers (NFOs) specifically because they carry better commission on new launches, switching customers between schemes without clear consent, and selling a regular plan to someone who explicitly asked for a direct one.

This is the distinction worth holding onto: the SEBI retention data above is a genuine, honest case for working with someone who knows you — not a blanket case for whatever your bank branch happens to be pushing this quarter. A trail-commission-based regular plan, recommended by someone who actually asked about your goals and will still take your call in a falling market, is a completely different product experience from a regular plan sold across a bank counter to hit a monthly target.

How to tell the difference, in practice

Ask directly whether the person recommending a fund earns a commission on it — a real advisor will tell you plainly. Be specifically cautious around New Fund Offers; an established fund with a real track record rarely needs to be "launched" to you urgently. And if a switch or a new purchase happens in your account that you don't remember authorising, that is not a grey area — file it on SEBI's SCORES portal (scores.gov.in).

The takeaway

SIP lifespan isn't mainly a math problem — it's a behavioural one. The data says having someone in your corner measurably improves the odds you stay invested long enough for compounding to actually work. The data also says that "someone" needs to be accountable to you, not to a sales target. Both things are true at once, and this week's number is a reminder to check which one you're actually getting.
